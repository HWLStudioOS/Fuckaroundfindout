## 5 · The Buyer's Eye — @creepersnursery (8 slides)
Monday Minute expertise. How to judge a specimen tree before you spec it.

- **Cover:** A BUYER'S EYE / READING A SPECIMEN TREE BEFORE YOU SPEC IT
- **Read the union first:** On a standard Carpinus betulus or Quercus you want one dominant central leader, not two stems meeting in a tight, bark-pinched fork that will split in twenty years. On a multi-stem Betula the rule inverts. There is no leader to want, so judge the stems for even weight and clean unions instead.
- **Then read the branching:** Good branches are spaced around the stem, not stacked or crossing, and divide into fine, repeated twig. That density of ramification is the quiet sign of a tree grown with room and time, not forced. Field Maple holds it naturally.
- **Make it stand alone:** A sound stem tapers from the base and stands vertical on its own. If a tree of decent girth still leans on its cane, it was drawn up too close to its neighbours and has no caliper to hold itself.
- **Ask about the rootball:** A specimen lifted and re-rooted every two to three years carries a firm, fibrous, well-furnished rootball in proportion to its crown. That ratio is what decides whether it establishes or sulks.
- **A feature bark is unforgiving:** On a feature bark like Prunus serrula or Betula utilis 'Jacquemontii' a clean stem is non-negotiable, because the bark is the feature. Old ties, scars and dieback show on the one thing the client will stand and look at.
- **Walk around it:** A one-sided crown means it was grown too tight in the row. Correct spacing in the field buys you an even canopy that reads from every approach, not just the one the photo was taken from.
- **Closer:** "Grown with room and time" / Thirty-eight years in the ground teaches you what to look for. DM the office and we will walk the rows with your plant list. / @creepersnursery · creepersnursery.co.uk

**Caption:**
Most specimen trees are bought on a photo and a price. The ones that establish well and read right on site were chosen on the things a photo cannot show.

Here is how we read a tree before it earns a spec. The form of the union. The spacing and ramification of the branches. The taper of the stem and whether it stands without its cane. The rootball it has built from regular lifting. The cleanliness of a feature bark. The evenness of the crown from every approach.

None of it is fast. A tree grown with room and time tells you so in the wood. Thirty-eight years in the ground teaches you to look for it.
