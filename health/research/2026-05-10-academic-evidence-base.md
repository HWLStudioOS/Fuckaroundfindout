# Academic Evidence Base for a 9-Week Build to a First 50K

**Compiled:** 10 May 2026
**Scope:** Peer-reviewed evidence only. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses prioritised over single trials. Recreational/sub-elite recreational athlete data flagged separately from elite extrapolation.
**Athlete profile (context, not optimisation target):** 25 y/o male, 6'1, ~85 kg, 1:45 HM PB, ~3,000 km lifetime running, 25-50 km/wk current base, lapsed strength base, recurrent ITB and patellar tendinopathy history, 9 weeks out from a UK summer 50K (~720 m gain, mostly runnable).

A note on evidence weight throughout: "strong" = multiple high-quality systematic reviews/meta-analyses converging; "moderate" = one good meta-analysis or several consistent RCTs; "weak" = single small trials, observational data, or contested findings.

---

## 1. Optimal Weekly Running Volume

**Headline:** The recreational/marathon literature shows a U-shaped curve: injury risk is *elevated* below ~30 km/wk and rises again at very high volumes, with the "protective" band sitting around 30-60 km/wk for marathoners. There is no good RCT evidence for a specific peak volume in a 9-week 50K build, and the often-cited "60-80 km peak" is coaching custom rather than data-derived. **Strength of evidence: moderate for the lower threshold; weak/absent for an upper threshold specific to first-time 50K runners.**

**Key studies:**

- **Rasmussen et al. (2013), J Strength Cond Res**, prospective cohort of 662 marathoners. Runners averaging <30 km/wk had a 1.34x relative risk of injury vs those at 30-60 km/wk. Sample n=662, recreational marathoners (population matches the subject). [PubMed 23593549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23593549/)
- **Scheer et al. (prospective ultra cohort, 2023)**, in ultramarathon trainees, the relationship between external load and injury was significant only for those running <30 km/wk. Injury frequency stabilised at ~5 sessions/week. [PMC9924505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9924505/)
- **Hulme et al. and follow-up GAM analysis (2025)**, the Generalised Additive Model approach showed training load–injury relationships in ultras are non-linear and individually variable; no clean weekly km cut-point was identifiable above the 30 km lower bound. [PMC12327882](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12327882/)
- **Damsted et al., umbrella review (2024)**, confirmed weekly mileage above ~40 mi/wk (~64 km) protects against injury in full marathoners; data thin above 80 km/wk. [ScienceDirect S2095254624000607](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254624000607)

**Contested:** Whether there is a true upper-bound injury threshold. Some observational data points to rising injury risk above ~100 km/wk, but confounded by self-selection (more durable runners run more).

**Where the evidence is weak/absent:** No RCTs prescribing specific peak weekly volumes for first-time 50K runners with a 25-50 km baseline. The 60-80 km coaching figure is not directly supported; it sits within the "protective" band identified for marathoners but is extrapolated.

**Practical interpretation for the subject:** Evidence supports staying ≥30 km/wk and progressing into the 50-65 km/wk range without strong injury signal, provided ACWR (see below) stays ≤1.3-1.5.

## 2. Intensity Distribution

**Headline:** When matched on time-in-zone, **polarised (POL)** and **pyramidal (PYR)** distributions produce *equivalent* VO2max and time-trial outcomes in trained runners. Both clearly outperform threshold-heavy training. The popular "POL is superior" claim is not supported by the most recent meta-analysis. **Strength of evidence: strong.**

**Key studies:**

- **Filipas et al. (2024), Sports Medicine**, meta-analysis of 13 studies / 348 recreational and competitive endurance athletes. Result: **no significant difference between POL and PYR in VO2max or TT performance** when intensity distributions were quantified by time-in-zone. POL > threshold-heavy. [Springer 10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z) / [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/)
- **Festa et al. (2022), J Strength Cond Res**, 16-week direct comparison of POL vs PYR in well-trained runners (n=29). Both improved 10K performance significantly; POL slightly favoured for sub-elite runners but the effect was small. [PubMed 34792817](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34792817/)
- **Treff et al. and earlier Stöggl & Sperlich (2014, 2019)**, showed POL > threshold for VO2max gains, establishing the original "POL is best" narrative that the 2024 meta-analysis nuanced.
- **Rosenblat et al. (2021/2022)**, POL vs threshold meta-analysis: POL produced larger gains in TT performance (effect size moderate). POL vs PYR comparison was non-significant. [ResearchGate 325492365](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325492365_Polarized_vs_Threshold_Training_Intensity_Distribution_on_Endurance_Sport_Performance_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_of_Randomized_Controlled_Trials)

**Contested:** Whether POL > PYR in trained vs untrained athletes. Some signal that more highly trained runners benefit slightly more from POL because they need the protected easy volume; recreational athletes do well on PYR.

**For a 9-week build:** Either POL (~80% Z1-2 / ~20% Z4-5) or PYR (~75% Z1-2 / ~20% Z3 / ~5% Z4-5) is evidence-defensible. Threshold-heavy ("sweet spot") training is the *worst* of the three for VO2max gains, though it can produce short-term TT improvements.

## 3. Strength Training Integration with Endurance

**Headline:** Concurrent training does **not** blunt endurance adaptations and improves running economy by 2-8% in trained runners. The interference effect runs the other way (endurance can blunt strength), and is mostly mitigated by separating sessions by ≥6 hours. Heavy load and plyometric work are the most potent for running economy; isometric is less effective for economy but has specific tendon-health benefits. **Strength of evidence: strong for endurance benefits; moderate for timing.**

**Key studies:**

- **Llanos-Lagos et al. (2024), Sports Medicine**, meta-analysis on TID + strength methods in middle/long-distance runners. **High-load strength training (>80% 1RM), plyometric, and combined methods improved running economy**. Submaximal load and isolated isometric were less effective for economy. [Springer 10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y) / [PMC11052887](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/)
- **Berryman et al. (2024), Sports Medicine**, concurrent strength+endurance meta-analysis, 59 studies, n=1,346. Concurrent training **blunted lower-body strength in males but not females**, and impaired power gains in both sexes. Endurance outcomes were **not** harmed. [PMC10933151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933151/)
- **Eddens et al. and the 2024 sequencing review**, separating concurrent sessions by ≥6 hours minimises acute interference; same-session sequence effects are small (negligible for endurance, small effect on strength). [Lippincott medicine.lww.com 2024](https://journals.lww.com/md-journal/fulltext/2024/12270/optimizing_concurrent_training_programs__a_review.22.aspx)
- **Beattie et al. (2017), J Strength Cond Res**, RCT in competitive distance runners: 40-week strength block improved RE and 5K performance without compositional change. (Specific landmark, see Beattie review in [Sports Medicine 2014, Beattie et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0157-y) ).

**Minimum effective dose for maintenance:**
- **2x/week, 2-3 compound lifts at ≥80% 1RM, 3-5 sets x 4-6 reps** is the consensus minimum effective dose during an endurance block (Llanos-Lagos 2024). Less than 1 session/week shows minimal effect.

**Tendon-specific loading (eccentric vs isometric vs concentric):**
- **Heavy slow resistance (HSR)** at 3 sec concentric/3 sec eccentric, 3 sets x 6-15 reps, 3x/week is the most evidence-supported modality for *long-term* patellar tendon adaptation (see Q7).
- **Isometric** (Spanish squat, leg-extension hold, 5 reps x 45 sec at 70% MVIC) produces short-term analgesia (~45 min) and is the best in-season tool. Mechanism is corticospinal disinhibition (Rio et al. 2015).
- **Eccentric** (Alfredson-style) is superseded by HSR for patellar tendon (it remains first-line for Achilles).

## 4. HRV-Guided Training Modulation

**Headline:** HRV-guided training produces a **small but positive effect on VO2max and TT performance vs fixed plans** in recreational and sub-elite athletes, but the absolute advantage is modest (effect sizes 0.2-0.5) and the *floor* for usable signal is **5+ measurements per week using rolling averages**. Single-night data should not drive day-to-day decisions. **Strength of evidence: moderate.**

**Key studies:**

- **Düking et al. (2021), Sports Med Open**, meta-analytic systematic review on HRV-guided training. Found a small positive effect on VO2max (SMD ~0.3) over fixed plans, conditioned on athlete level and sex. Vagal HRV indices improved more reliably than performance. [PMC8507742](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8507742/)
- **Granero-Gallegos et al. (2020), Int J Environ Res Public Health**, RCT in professional endurance runners (n=12) showed HRV-guided > fixed plan on TT performance. Small sample. [PubMed 32751204](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32751204/)
- **Manresa-Rocamora et al. (2021), Sports Medicine**, HRV-based for VO2max meta-analysis: positive effect, conditioned on sex and training level. Limited transferability to elite. [PMC7663087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7663087/)
- **Plews, Buchheit et al. (2012), Eur J Appl Physiol**, landmark methodology paper establishing **7-day rolling average ln rMSSD** as the working signal; coefficient of variation (CV) of the 7-day average is the more sensitive marker, with rising CV preceding non-functional overreaching. [PubMed 22367011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22367011/)

**Operational specifics with citation support:**
- **Decision rule used in HRV-guided RCTs:** if morning ln rMSSD falls outside the smallest worthwhile change (SWC) of the rolling 7-day baseline (typically ±0.5 × within-subject SD, often operationalised as ~3-5%), the planned high-intensity session is replaced with low-intensity. Hard threshold values (5%, 7%) are not directly RCT-derived; they are practitioner heuristics. The SWC approach is what the literature actually supports.
- **Floor for usable signal:** Plews and colleagues' work shows that **single-day HRV scores don't predict training response**; weekly averages do. Practical floor is 5 measurements/week to estimate a rolling mean; <3/week is essentially noise.
- **Acute confounders with quantified effect:** Acute alcohol drops next-morning ln rMSSD by ~10-15% (a confound exceeding any meaningful training signal). Travel ≥3 timezones, illness, and acute mental stress all displace rMSSD outside SWC for 24-72 hours. None of these reflect training need.

**Where the evidence is weak/absent:** No high-quality data on HRV-guided protocols for ultramarathon-specific build phases. Most trials are 4-12 weeks, not 9-week ultra builds. The threshold figures (>5%, >7%) circulating in coaching content are extrapolations from SWC methodology, not directly tested.

## 5. Race-Pace Specificity

**Headline:** Strong evidence in the marathon literature supports race-pace specificity in the **final 6-8 weeks** at ~15-20% of weekly volume; ultramarathon-specific RCT data is essentially absent. For a 9-week build, weeks 4-7 are the right window for race-pace-equivalent (RPE-equivalent or HR-equivalent) work. **Strength of evidence: strong for marathon, weak/extrapolated for ultra.**

**Key studies:**

- **Daniels' Running Formula (Daniels & Gilbert, foundational and updated)**, established the marathon-pace volume guideline of 15-20% of weekly mileage during the 10-12 week race-specific block. Not an RCT, but the underlying VDOT framework has been validated in observational studies.
- **Casado et al. (2021/2022), Frontiers in Physiology**, review of training characteristics in elite distance runners. Race-pace volume in the specific phase clusters around 15-25% of total volume. [Frontiers fphys 2024.1446837](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1446837/full)
- **Stellingwerff et al. (review on long-distance carbohydrate strategy, 2025)**, discusses specificity in the context of fuelling rehearsal, recommending race-pace efforts paired with race-day fuelling protocol in the 4-6 weeks pre-race. [PMC11901785](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11901785/)
- **No specific 50K race-pace meta-analysis exists.** The transferable principle from marathon literature is concentrated specificity in the final 4-6 weeks.

**Block vs spread:** Marathon literature mildly favours **concentrated specificity** (back-to-back race-pace long runs in the peak weeks) over micro-dosing across the build, because the cumulative metabolic stimulus matters more than frequency. Volume per week at race pace in the final 4-6 weeks: target 12-25 km cumulative race-pace running per week embedded in long runs (8-15 km block at goal pace at the back end of a long run is the highest-yield format).

**For a 7:30-8:00/km target:** This is well below the subject's HM-derived threshold pace, so "race-pace" specificity for ultras is more about *time-on-feet at the right RPE* and *fuelling rehearsal* than about hitting a specific pace. The 50K-relevant specificity is probably better framed as 4-5 hour long runs at goal RPE rather than km-precise pacing work.

## 6. Taper Science

**Headline:** Optimal taper for endurance events: **2 weeks (8-14 days)**, exponential volume reduction of **41-60%**, **no change to intensity or frequency**. Expected performance gain ~1.5-3%. **Strength of evidence: strong.**

**Key studies:**

- **Bosquet et al. (2007), Med Sci Sports Exerc**, original taper meta-analysis (27 studies). Concluded: **2-week taper, exponential volume reduction of 41-60%, intensity and frequency unchanged** maximises performance. Mean gain ~1.96% in performance markers. [PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)
- **Spilsbury et al. and the 2023 PLOS ONE update by Andrade et al.**, systematic review and meta-analysis on tapering in endurance athletes. Confirmed the Bosquet findings: **largest TT improvement at 8-14 days taper, 41-60% volume reduction, intensity maintained**. Short tapers (≤7 days) and longer tapers (15-21 days) also work but with smaller effect sizes. [PMC10171681](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10171681/)
- **Mujika et al.**, methodological reviews supporting the same operational pattern.

**Specific operational answers:**
- **Length:** 2 weeks > 1 week > 3 weeks (effect sizes for endurance: ES ~0.32-0.59 at 8-14 days)
- **Volume curve:** Exponential or step-decay; both better than linear in the meta-analytic data
- **Intensity:** **Preserve fully**, reducing intensity nullifies the taper benefit (Andrade 2023)
- **Frequency:** Maintain (don't drop session count)
- **Magnitude of expected gain:** ~1.5-3% improvement in TT-type performance

**For a 5-7 hour 50K, the same principles apply but with one caveat:** ultra-distance taper benefits are less directly measured. The standard 2-week taper applies; a slightly longer 3-week taper has weak support for events >4 hours due to the additional structural recovery required. For a first-timer, a clean 14-day taper with intensity preserved (e.g., one short tempo + strides in race week) is the safest evidence-supported choice.

## 7. Patellar Tendinopathy and ITB Syndrome

**Headline (patellar):** **Heavy slow resistance (HSR) is best for long-term outcomes; isometrics are best for in-season pain control; eccentric-only protocols are no longer first-line.** **Headline (ITB):** **Hip abductor strengthening + gait retraining (cadence increase ~5-10%) is the strongest evidence-based combination.** **Strength of evidence: moderate for both.**

**Patellar tendinopathy, key studies:**

- **Liao et al. (2024), Heliyon**, network meta-analysis comparing eccentric, isometric, and HSR for VISA-P score in adults with patellar tendinopathy. **HSR best for long-term knee function**; **eccentric-only was the worst** of the three for VISA-P improvement; **isometric + progressive tendon loading > eccentric alone**. [Heliyon S2405-8440(24)15202-4](https://www.cell.com/heliyon/fulltext/S2405-8440(24)15202-4) / [PubMed 39559237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39559237/)
- **Rio et al. (2015), Br J Sports Med**, landmark RCT showing **5x45 sec isometric quadriceps holds at 70% MVIC produce immediate analgesia (~45 min)** in patellar tendinopathy via cortical disinhibition. Sample n=6 elite jumpers in original study; replicated in larger cohorts. [PubMed 25979840](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25979840/)
- **2024 systematic review (Frontiers Rehabil Sciences)**, confirmed isometric is superior for short-term acute pain relief (analgesia within 45 min); HSR superior for long-term VISA-P gains. [PMC12259690](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12259690/)

**Practical patellar protocol with citation support:**
- In-season acute flare: **Spanish squat or leg-extension isometric hold, 5 reps x 45 sec at 70% MVIC, 4-6 sets, daily** (Rio 2015)
- Long-term tendon loading: **HSR, squat/leg press at 70-85% 1RM, 3 sec concentric/3 sec eccentric, 3 sets x 6-15 reps, 3x/week, 12 weeks**

**ITB syndrome, key studies:**

- **Friede et al. (2024), Frontiers in Sports and Active Living**, systematic review of conservative ITBS treatment in 13 studies / 201 runners. Hip abductor strengthening was a **key component** of every successful protocol. Combined hip strengthening + myofascial release best for pain/function at 6 weeks. **22 of 24 athletes pain-free and back to running by 6 weeks; no recurrences at 6 months.** [Frontiers fspor 2024.1386456](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1386456/full) / [PMC11377285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11377285/)
- **Fredericson et al. (2000)**, original abductor weakness study showing 34.9% (F) / 51.4% (M) increase in hip abductor torque after rehab predicted return-to-running. Sample n=24, distance runners. [PubMed 10959926](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10959926/)
- **Willy et al. and others on gait retraining**, increasing cadence by 5-10% reduces ITB strain significantly. Single-step gait modification adds incremental benefit on top of strengthening.

**Time-course and return-to-run markers:**
- **Patellar (HSR protocol):** symptomatic improvement at 4 weeks, structural at 12 weeks. VISA-P >80 and pain ≤3/10 on single-leg decline squat are the practical RTR markers.
- **ITBS:** 4-6 weeks to symptom resolution with adherent strengthening + gait change. Pain-free single-leg step-down and pain-free 30-min run are the practical RTR markers.

**Contested:** Whether hip abductor weakness is *cause* or *consequence* of ITBS. Some studies find equivalent abductor strength between ITBS and healthy runners (Friede 2024 notes the mixed evidence). The intervention works regardless; the mechanism is debated.

## 8. Heat Acclimatisation

**Headline:** For a UK July race in 15-25°C conditions, **the standard "10-14 daily heat sessions over 2 weeks" protocol is well-supported, but post-exercise sauna or hot-water immersion produces equivalent thermoregulatory adaptation with less training disruption**. Adaptation decay is faster than commonly cited: meaningful loss begins within 1 week and ~75% lost by 3 weeks. **Strength of evidence: strong on protocol; moderate on decay rates.**

**Key studies:**

- **Daanen, Racinais & Périard (2018), Sports Medicine**, **definitive systematic review and meta-analysis on heat acclimation decay and re-induction**. Key numbers: 1 day of HA is lost per 2 days of decay; HR adaptations decay ~2.5%/day; ~75% of adaptation lost by ~28 days. [PMC5775394](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775394/) / [PubMed 29129022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29129022/)
- **Tyler et al. (2025), Temperature**, quantitative meta-regression on protocol characteristics and adaptation kinetics. Confirms 10-14 sessions as optimal; diminishing returns past 14. [PMC12122934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12122934/)
- **Zurawlew et al. (2018, 2019), Frontiers Physiol / Eur J Appl Physiol**, post-exercise hot-water immersion (40°C for 40 min on 6 consecutive days) produced equivalent thermoregulatory adaptation to traditional HA. Adaptations retained for ≥2 weeks. Sample n=17 endurance-trained, recreational. [PMC6305481](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6305481/)
- **Stanley et al. (2015), and Pilch et al. and others on sauna**, post-exercise sauna 30 min at ~80-90°C for 10-12 sessions over 3 weeks produced ~32% increase in time-to-exhaustion and meaningful plasma volume expansion. [PMC7862510](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7862510/)

**Operational answers:**
- **Protocol type:** Active HA (heat sessions during running) is gold standard; post-exercise hot-water immersion or sauna is the practical alternative with equivalent adaptation. Sauna alone (without prior exercise) is less effective.
- **Session count:** 10-14 sessions is optimal; 5-6 sessions captures most cardiovascular adaptation; >14 sessions = diminishing returns
- **Duration:** 30-40 min per session (below this, thermal load is insufficient)
- **When to start:** **Start ~2 weeks pre-race, finish 3-4 days pre-race** to avoid acute fatigue and limit decay window
- **Decay rate:** ~2.5% per day for HR adaptations; ~75% lost by 3 weeks of no exposure (Daanen 2018)

**For a UK race in only 15-25°C:** Ergogenic benefit of HA is **smaller in moderate heat** than in extreme heat; the strongest case for HA is plasma volume expansion (a general aerobic performance enhancer that helps even in mild conditions). 6-10 post-exercise sauna sessions in the 2-3 weeks pre-race is a high-yield, low-disruption protocol for this race profile.

## 9. Carb Loading and During-Race Fuelling

**Headline:** Carb-load with **8-12 g/kg/day for 24-36 hours pre-race**; during-race intake of **60-90 g/hr using 2:1 glucose:fructose** is the consensus ceiling for most athletes; **120 g/hr is achievable in elite mountain runners with gut training but not standard**; **caffeine 3-6 mg/kg taken 60 min pre-race produces a 2-7% endurance benefit**. **Strength of evidence: strong for carb dose during race; strong for caffeine; moderate for sodium individualisation.**

**Key studies:**

- **Burke et al. (current ISSN/IOC consensus) and Stellingwerff (2025)**, pre-race carb-load: **8-12 g/kg/day** for 24-36 h pre-race, low-fibre in the 24-h window. Above 12 g/kg/day adds GI distress without adding usable glycogen. [PMC11901785](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11901785/)
- **Jeukendrup and the multi-transportable carbohydrate literature**, intestinal SGLT1 saturates at ~60 g/hr glucose; adding fructose (GLUT5) raises ceiling to **90-120 g/hr** with 2:1 or 1:0.8 glucose:fructose. [GSSI Jeukendrup SSE 178](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/training-the-gut-for-athletes)
- **Viribay et al. (2020), Nutrients**, **120 g/hr in elite mountain marathoners** reduced exercise-induced muscle damage markers vs 60 or 90 g/hr. Population: elite, n=20, with prior gut training. **Not generalisable to first-time ultra runners.**
- **Southward et al. (2018), Sports Medicine**, caffeine endurance meta-analysis: **3-6 mg/kg, 60 min pre-exercise, improves endurance running performance by 2-7%**. [Frontiers fspor 2022.821750](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.821750/full)
- **Guest et al. (2021), ISSN Position Stand on Caffeine**, confirmed 3-6 mg/kg as ergogenic dose; minimal effective dose may be ~2 mg/kg; doses >9 mg/kg show no added benefit and increase side effects. [PubMed 33388079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/)
- **NATA position statement (Casa et al.) and ACSM**, sodium **0.3-0.7 g/L of fluid for events >1 hr; consider 0.5-0.7 g/L for events >4 hr**. Individual sweat sodium varies widely (200-2,000 mg/L); the population mean is ~1,000 mg/L. [NATA Fluid Replacement 2017](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/fluid_replacement_for_the_physically_active.pdf)
- **Costa et al. (2017, 2023), 'Train the Gut' systematic literature review**, gut training requires 8-10 weeks of regular high-carb feeding for full mechanical (volume) tolerance; first transport adaptations within 10-14 days. Improves *tolerance*, not absolute absorption capacity. [PMC10185635](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10185635/)

**Specific operational answers:**

| Domain | Recommendation | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Carb load (24-36h pre) | 8-12 g/kg/day, low-fibre | Burke / Stellingwerff |
| Race-day pre-race meal (1-4h) | 1-4 g/kg | Burke / ISSN |
| In-race carbs | 60-90 g/hr; 2:1 glucose:fructose | Jeukendrup |
| Trained-gut ceiling | 120 g/hr (elite, with 8-10 wk gut training) | Viribay 2020 |
| Sodium (moderate sweat) | 500-700 mg/hr fluid; individualise | NATA / ACSM |
| Caffeine | 3-6 mg/kg, ~60 min pre-race | Southward / ISSN 2021 |
| Caffeine "top up" mid-race | 1-2 mg/kg every 90-120 min, evidence weaker for ultra | ISSN 2021 |
| Gut training timeline | 8-10 wk for full tolerance; 10-14 days for first adaptations | Costa 2017/2023 |

**For a 5-7 hour effort:** The realistic intake is **60-80 g/hr** for a first-time ultra runner without a long gut-training history. Pushing to 90 g/hr requires deliberate practice in long runs over 6-8 weeks. **Fuelling rehearsal in long runs is non-negotiable**, most ultra DNFs are GI-related, not aerobic.

## 10. Recovery Between Sessions

**Headline:** **CWI does not blunt aerobic adaptation but does blunt strength/hypertrophy adaptation**, safe for recovery during an endurance build, problematic if also trying to build muscle. **Sauna for recovery and sauna for heat acclimation are not in conflict at moderate doses.** **Sleep extension to ~46-113 min above habitual baseline** is the most reliable recovery intervention. **Protein 0.4 g/kg post-long-run maximises myofibrillar synthesis.** **Strength of evidence: strong on CWI, sleep, and protein; moderate on sauna.**

**Key studies:**

- **Malta et al. (2021), Sports Medicine**, meta-analysis on regular CWI use: **no negative effect on aerobic performance** (no significant change in TT or VO2max), but moderate negative effects on 1RM and isometric strength. [PubMed 33146851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33146851/)
- **Piñero et al. (2024), Eur J Sport Sci**, meta-analysis confirming CWI attenuates resistance-training-induced hypertrophy. [Wiley 10.1002/ejsc.12074](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsc.12074)
- **CWI dose-response network meta-analysis (2025)**, shorter immersions (≤12 min) at colder temperatures (≤12-13°C) optimise CK reduction and endurance recovery. [PMC11897523](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11897523/)
- **Bonnar et al. and the Cunha et al. (2023) sleep extension meta-analysis**, **extending sleep by 46-113 min above habitual** improves athletic performance (sprint, reaction time, sustained endurance). [PMC10354314](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10354314/)
- **Churchward-Venne et al. (2020), Am J Clin Nutr**, RCT showing **30 g (or ~0.49 g/kg) of protein maximises myofibrillar protein synthesis after endurance exercise**. Doses above this don't add further synthesis benefit. [PubMed 32359142](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32359142/)
- **Jäger et al. (2017), ISSN Position Stand on Protein**, **0.25-0.4 g/kg every 3-4 hours** for endurance athletes during recovery; total daily 1.4-2.0 g/kg. [PMC5477153](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/)

**Specific operational answers:**

- **CWI in a build phase:** Acceptable for endurance recovery (no aerobic interference). Skip on heavy strength days if the strength session was the primary stimulus you want to keep.
- **Sauna for recovery vs sauna for heat acclimation:** Not in conflict. Post-exercise sauna 20-30 min serves both. Conflict only arises if cold-exposure is added immediately after sauna (which blunts the heat adaptation signal).
- **Sleep duration floor for endurance recovery:** Studies converge on **8-10 hours/night** during heavy training blocks; sleep extension trials show benefit when athletes habitually sleep 7 hours. <7 hours/night impairs aerobic endurance and accelerates glycogen depletion.
- **Protein post-long-run:** **30-40 g (≈0.4 g/kg for the 85 kg subject)** within 1-2 hours. Whey or equivalent leucine-rich protein. Co-ingest with carbs (1.0-1.2 g/kg/hr carb in the first 4 h post-run) for glycogen resynthesis.

**Contested:** Whether the "anabolic window" exists at all (some evidence it's a 24-hour window, not a 30-min window). Practically, 30-40 g protein within 1-2 hours of a long run is uncontroversial.
