## Caption

Most specimen trees are bought on a photo and a price. The ones that establish well and read properly on site were chosen on the things a photograph cannot show.

Start with the branch unions. Read the spacing and balance of the crown. Check that the stem tapers and stands without relying on its cane. Ask how the rootball has been prepared. On a feature-bark tree, look at the stem itself. Then walk the full circle.

None of it is fast. A tree grown with room and time shows it in the wood.

If you are sourcing semi-mature stock for a scheme, send the office your plant list and we will walk the rows with you.

## Slide copy

1. **A Buyer's Eye**
   Reading a specimen tree before you spec it.
2. **Read the union first**
   Start at the point where the main branches meet. On a single-stem tree, look for a clear leader and well-spaced unions. On a multi-stem, look for even weight and clean joins.
3. **Then read the branching**
   Good branches are spread around the stem rather than stacked on one side. Fine repeated twigging is the quiet sign of a tree given room and time.
4. **Make it stand alone**
   The stem should taper from the base and hold itself upright. A strong specimen should not depend on its cane for shape.
5. **Ask about the rootball**
   A well-prepared specimen carries a firm, fibrous rootball in proportion to its crown. That balance is central to how it settles after lifting.
6. **A feature bark is unforgiving**
   On *Prunus serrula* or *Betula utilis* 'Jacquemontii', the stem is the feature. Check for clean bark, old tie marks, damage and dieback.
7. **Walk around it**
   A tree has more than one front. Walk the full circle and check that the crown is balanced from every approach.
8. **Grown with room and time**
   Thirty-eight years in the ground teaches you what to look for. DM the office and we will walk the rows with your plant list.
