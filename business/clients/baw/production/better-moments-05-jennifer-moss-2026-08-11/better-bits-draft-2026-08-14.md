### Draft

Hi,

Hope can sound like the thing you reach for when there is no plan.

Jennifer Moss uses it differently. Drawing on psychologist Charles Snyder's hope theory, she treats hope as something built from three parts:

1. A goal worth reaching.
2. More than one credible pathway to get there.
3. Enough agency to actually move.

Take any one of those away and hope stops being a plan. It just becomes a feeling.

That model explains two things a lot of teams are living through right now.

After a round of layoffs, the people who stay often go quiet on risk, not because they are short on ideas, but because they cannot see a secure path forward for themselves. Under a micromanager, someone can have the goal and the route mapped out for them and still have no agency left. Jennifer's point is that this is what quietly drains accomplishment, autonomy and intrinsic motivation from people who still care about the work.

Her smallest recommendation is also the easiest place to start this week. When the big goal feels too far off, shrink the next one until it is small enough to finish today, and make that progress visible.

Three questions worth putting to your team this week:

- What is the next goal we can actually finish?
- Where is our plan B, if plan A stalls?
- Which decision could move a step closer to the person doing the work?

We cut Jennifer's full conversation into one seven-minute Better Moment. It covers the hope model, the hope gap opening up for younger workers, and what micromanagement quietly takes away.
