## Slides

1. **Cover:** newspaper clipping on green / THE PEAT QUESTION / AS FEATURED IN THE SUNDAY TIMES · 12 JULY 2026
2. **Why peat was everywhere** — For decades peat was the default growing medium. It holds moisture, promotes strong root runs, particularly for seedlings, and is light to handle. Most of British horticulture was built on it.
3. **The real cost** — Peatlands are among Britain's largest carbon stores and richest wildlife habitats. Digging them up for compost releases carbon that took thousands of years to lay down.
4. **What replaces it** — Peat-free composts are made from composted bark, wood fibre, sawdust and paper waste. They improve every season, but they are not yet a like-for-like swap for every crop.
5. **Why the switch is hard** — Most young plants are propagated before they ever reach a British nursery, many of them abroad, and most start life in peat. A grower is only as peat-free as its supply chain.
6. **Twenty-six suppliers. Three peat-free.** — For Chelsea 2026 we worked with 26 suppliers. Many had reduced their peat. Only three had removed it entirely. That is the honest state of the supply chain today.
7. **A phased transition** — "It's like banning petrol cars and saying everyone has to be driving electric vehicles, but saying it has to happen next year, without putting in place the infrastructure … It has to be a phased transition." Michael Buck, Head of Horticulture, in The Sunday Times.
8. **Fifteen years of deadlines** — A voluntary phase-out was agreed in 2011, for 2020. In 2021 a ban on bagged peat was promised by 2024. The professional deadline moved to 2026 and passed again. There is still no law.
9. **Where we stand** — We are committed to growing peat-free, and honest about what today's supply chain can guarantee. Rather than compromise the standard, we are pausing Chelsea for a year and backing a phased transition with real infrastructure behind it.
