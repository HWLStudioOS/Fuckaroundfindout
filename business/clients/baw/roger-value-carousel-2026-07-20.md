## Slide copy

### 01

ROGER MARTIN / STRATEGY

**5 QUESTIONS THAT TURN A PLAN INTO A STRATEGY**

Five connected choices. One way to test them.

A PRACTICAL FRAMEWORK

### 02

01 / START WITH THE OUTCOME

**What is our winning aspiration?**

Name the outcome you are trying to create. Not the project. Not the revenue target. The meaningful win that gives every other choice a direction.

**Quick check:** If the answer sounds like a KPI, keep going.

### 03

02 / CHOOSE THE FIELD

**Where will we play?**

Choose the customers, problems, channels and geographies that matter. A real choice should make it clear where you will not play.

**The test:** Can the team name the places you have deliberately ruled out?

### 04

03 / NAME THE ADVANTAGE

**How will we win there?**

Name the advantage that makes people choose you. “Better service” and “great people” are rarely distinctive enough on their own.

**Press on the answer:** If a competitor could say the same thing, it is not an advantage yet.

### 05

04 / BUILD THE ABILITY

**What must we be unusually good at?**

Identify the two or three abilities the organisation must perform consistently for the first three choices to be credible.

**Keep it tight:** If everything is a capability, nothing is.

### 06

05 / MAKE IT REPEATABLE

**What systems make it real?**

Measures, routines, incentives and decisions turn capability into behaviour. If the systems reward the opposite, the strategy loses.

**Check the system:** Does it reward the behaviour your strategy actually needs?

### 07

THE TEST

**What would have to be true?**

Every choice rests on assumptions. Put them on paper, rank them by risk, then test the weakest one first.

**Do this next:** Write the five answers on one page. Circle the assumption you trust least. Test that before polishing the plan.

## Caption

Most teams can show you their plan.

Fewer can explain the choices underneath it.

Here is a useful test for your next strategy meeting:

Give everyone five minutes to answer these questions separately.

1. What is our winning aspiration?
2. Where will we play?
3. How will we win there?
4. What must we be unusually good at?
5. What systems will make it real?

Then compare the answers.

If you get five different versions, you do not have an alignment problem. Your strategy is not clear enough yet.

One final question helps turn the disagreement into something useful:

What would have to be true?

Write down the assumptions underneath each answer. Find the one you trust least. Test that before adding another initiative to the plan.
