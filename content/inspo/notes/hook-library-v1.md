## Pattern 1. THE LIFE-AUDIT OPEN (Dan Jones)

Reference: DWvblhciNQp, 59s, 5918 likes.

**The verbatim:**
> So I'm 31 years old and my life hasn't panned out quite how I thought it would up to this point. Still not bought a gaff, no kids, not married and technically unemployed.

**Architecture:**
1. Age + the gap. ("I'm X and I'm not where I thought I'd be.")
2. The list of expected milestones, ticked off as misses. Three to four items, fast.
3. The self-deprecating frame ("on paper, a little bit of a dosser").
4. Pivot: but here's the thing I actually chose / the bet I took.
5. The lesson: you have to take the risks to get where you want to be.
6. Close on the unconventional path being worth it.

**Why this is your template:**
You said this verbatim. "I'm 26 and not where I thought I'd be. Did well in school, got the degree, got the job, moved across the world..." That's the architecture, beat for beat. Your version trades the dosser frame for the operator frame, but the move is identical.

**Variation for you:**
> I'm twenty-six and I'm not where I thought I'd be. Did well in school. Got the degree. Got the job. Crossed the world for a bigger version of it. Doubled the income. Sitting in rooms I used to read about. And the same nagging feeling I had at fifteen.

## Pattern 2. THE IDENTITY QUESTION (Tom Flower)

Reference: DXOghyskcds, 80s, 12,898 likes. THE Tom Flower piece you flagged for pinned-post envy.

**The verbatim opening:**
> My least favourite question to be asked is "Where are you from?" Do you mean my background? My accent? Where I live? Or what the hell I'm drinking? Because every question will give you a different answer.

**Architecture:**
1. Name a question you get asked all the time. Specific. (Where are you from. What do you do. Why don't you just get a job.)
2. Show why the question is unanswerable in one sentence.
3. Story-mode shift: "G'day, my name is Tom..." Suddenly we're inside the story.
4. Lineage and setting (parents, place, food, specifics).
5. Pattern of internal contradiction.
6. The geographic distance moment (he's filming "from Western Australia, 4,000 kilometres from my family"). This is the perspective frame.
7. The realisation. ("Home was never a place, it was a feeling.")
8. Callback to the opening question, now answered differently.

**Why this is your template for Pin 2:**
You're a Kiwi in London. You get asked "where are you from?" constantly and the answer keeps mattering more or less depending on the room. Devonport. Auckland. London. Where's home? This is your Pin 2 structurally, the "where I came from" piece, but built around a recurring question instead of a linear timeline.

**Variation for you:**
> The question I get asked most in London is "so when are you going back?" And every time it lands different. Sometimes it's tomorrow. Sometimes it's never. Sometimes it's the only question that matters and I don't have an answer. [Then Devonport, the affluence-from-the-outside, the leap, the chase.]

## Pattern 3. THE TIMESKIP REWIND (Tom Flower, switch-off piece)

Reference: DYWhIpghytZ, 78s, 234 likes (lower performance, but the structure is excellent).

**The verbatim:**
> Six months ago I disappeared, no reception, no posting, no internet, and it was the best thing I've ever done for my content. Let me rewind. Because six months ago I was doing everything right, posting every day, always online, always creating, and I was completely empty.

**Architecture:**
1. Sentence with a specific time anchor + counterintuitive claim.
2. The two-word reset: "Let me rewind." (Beat. Cut.)
3. Show the before-state. Then the breaking point.
4. The catalyst event (the 3,000km drive).
5. The realisation in the middle of it.
6. The lesson, stated clean.
7. The instruction to the viewer (here it's "switch off this weekend"). Earned because the whole piece set it up.

**When you'd use this:**
For any field-note where the counterintuitive claim is the hook. "Two weeks ago I stopped using AI for the first time in eight months and it was the best decision of the quarter." Then rewind.

## Pattern 4. THE SEVEN-YEAR CONFESSION (Kirk Lepiten)

Reference: DW1zYyEERei, 136s, 3681 likes.

**The verbatim:**
> I spent seven years working for a big YouTuber, a big creator, helping him build his brand. And it was a good gig. It was a fun gig. It paid well. I was super comfortable, super comfortable. And because it was a good gig in the creative, creative space, I told myself that I didn't need to do any of this for myself because I'm already living it through somebody else.

**Architecture:**
1. Specific duration + position. (Seven years. For someone.)
2. Repeat-for-emphasis ("super comfortable, super comfortable"), verbal tic that signals authenticity.
3. The self-deception named clearly. ("I told myself X.")
4. The questions you were asking yourself. ("Do I even have the skills?")
5. The line that broke it ("I can see there's a hunger inside of you").
6. The transition. The new mistake (freelance, still building for clients).
7. The leap. (Now making your own thing.)
8. Close on a quotable line: "Maybe safe is another word for stagnant."

**Critical observation for you:**
Kirk's opening is structurally IDENTICAL to your v0 draft. You wrote: "I spent years in house honing my craft with one of these, one of these, one of these." That's Kirk's "seven years working for a big YouTuber" + a visual gag. Same hook family. Your Pin 1 v1 incorporates this directly.

**The Kirk close is the model for your "the chase, in public" payoff:** "Maybe safe is another word for stagnant" lands as a line because the whole piece earned the right to say it. Your version needs the same earning.

## Pattern 5. THE VIVID SCENE → WISDOM (The Jaunt)

Reference: DW6NW9-kjWC, 60s, 4570 likes.

**The verbatim:**
> Self-employment can be such an insane lifestyle mode. You almost feel like an aristocrat. It's 2 p.m. on a Wednesday, and you're in flip flops in a baseball cap, bouncing your knee as you read Tolstoy and sip coffee from this little riverside cafe.

**Architecture:**
1. One-line claim. ("X is wild.")
2. Specific scene that makes the claim physical. Sensory details: time, day, clothes, posture, what they're drinking, what they're reading.
3. The line that twists it: "The mug is not just that you're here reading a book while someone else is working. The mug is that you managed to get there by doing something you don't hate."
4. Pivot to the warning: don't pick the obvious quick path.
5. The unexpected lesson (pick the thing you love that you assume can't pay).

**When you'd use this:**
For lifestyle-tier field notes where you want to seduce before you teach. The mistake creators make is leading with the lesson. The Jaunt leads with the scene and the lesson lands because we're already in the moment.

**Variation for you:**
> It's Tuesday morning at the running track, ten degrees, raining, no music. I'm running 800-metre repeats, alone. There's no client expecting this. There's no algorithm watching. There's just the watch and the next interval. And this is the work I'm most jealous of when other people show it. So I'm starting to show mine. [Then the field-note thesis.]

## Pattern 6. THE DEMONSTRATION OPEN (Tom Noske, $4M exercise)

Reference: DXRZEXGk7G2, 48s, 3636 likes.

**The verbatim:**
> I want to take home $4 million. I want to work four days a week. I want to work six hours a day. I want to start at eight AM. I want to finish at two. I want to take two holidays per year, four weeks each.

**Architecture:**
1. State the wild number / outcome in the first line. No context, no preamble.
2. List the constraints as fast statements (each clause a new line in subtitle).
3. Do the maths on camera. Real numbers, no rounding.
4. The reveal of the punchline number.
5. Reframe: it's not "go bigger," it's "this is the only thing that gets you near it."

**When you'd use this:**
For any piece where the structure is "let me show you my actual reasoning." The maths in public format. Calculator on screen, working visible. Trust through transparency.

**Variation for you:**
You doing this same exercise for HWL Studio, live, with the actual numbers. Or: "I just opened my project tracking for May. Here's how many client hours, how many self hours, how many hours of dead time. Let me show you what that maps to."

## Pattern 7. THE GOAL AUTOPSY (Tom Noske, million-dollar piece)

Reference: DW-pV3WE8ya, 172s, 2714 likes.

**The verbatim:**
> I've had this arbitrary goal of making a million dollars in a month for a long time now and I had this funny experience recently where I began to realize that that might be a bit of a stupid goal.

**Architecture:**
1. Confess the goal you had. Be specific. Use the actual number.
2. The "funny experience" frame ("I had this funny experience recently..."), this is the pre-hook that says trust me, this isn't a flex piece.
3. The exercise (what does the life actually require).
4. The reveal: the real number is much lower.
5. The reason this matters: when you chase the wrong number, you make trade-offs that defeat the original purpose.
6. Close on the strategic conclusion ("anything beyond what supports my team and supports my lifestyle is just ego").

**When you'd use this:**
For any piece where the structure is "I was chasing the wrong thing, here's what I learned." Reflection-tier content. Lower stakes than the demonstration open; higher trust.

## Pattern 8. THE FILM ESSAY (Calder Dune)

Reference: DW63qyOkXMz, 179s, 11030 likes. This is your Tier B template.

**The verbatim opening (after 30s of sourced film clips with score):**
> Power is a magnetic plague, and I think movies like Dune and Oppenheimer have come at the perfect time.

**Architecture:**
1. 20-30 seconds of pure film + score, no voiceover. The clips do the seducing.
2. ONE thesis line stated cleanly. ("Power is a magnetic plague.")
3. Connect the thesis to the films. ("Oppenheimer and Paul hold up an interesting mirror for these men.")
4. Personal observation embedded ("as I sit with the world and take a long look at the men around me...").
5. The reframe ("the measure of a man is not found at the peaks of his life, but in the valley between them...").
6. The wish for the audience. ("My wish for the generation of men around me is to not chase the peaks...")
7. Land on a question that has already been answered ("Do you wrestle with dreams? Do you contend with shadows?").

**When you'd use this:**
Your *Do You Hear The Music?* piece is exactly this. Open with the Oppenheimer clip + score. Thesis line: "There's a song the world is playing." Build it like Calder Dune built his Meditation on Men.

**Edit pace from this reel:**
- First 30s: 4-6 second holds on clips
- Voiceover sections: 2-3 second cuts, mostly matching the cadence of a sentence
- Title cards: 2-beat holds

## Cross-pattern observations

- **Specific numbers always.** Seven years. 4,000 km. $278,000. 31. 26. Three retainer clients. Specificity is the credibility.
- **Use the word "rewind" or "let me rewind."** Tom Flower uses it twice across two pieces. It earns a beat and a cut.
- **The repeat-for-emphasis tic.** "Super comfortable, super comfortable." Lands as authenticity, not laziness. Don't overdo.
- **The "on paper" frame.** "On paper, a little bit of a dosser." Self-deprecating but specific. Inverts status before you build it back up.
- **The close is almost never a CTA.** It's a quotable line or an answered question. Engagement bait kills the piece.
- **The hook usually contains the entire essay in compressed form.** "I'm 31 and not where I thought I'd be" is the whole reel. The other 50 seconds expand it.

## What to do with this file

When you sit down to draft a new script:
1. Pick the pattern that fits the energy (life-audit for personal arcs, scene-to-wisdom for lifestyle, demonstration for systems, film-essay for taste pieces).
2. Borrow the architecture exactly.
3. Fill it with your own specifics. Real names, real numbers, real moments.
4. Write the close last and make it earn the line.

When in doubt, Pattern 1 (life-audit) is yours. You have the life arc to power it for the next ten years.
