What your office is telling your team

## Draft

Hi,

Every big company I worked in had a mission statement that said we want your ideas.

Then you looked at the floor plan.

Leidy Klotz, an engineering professor and behavioural scientist, put a question to me on the show that I have not been able to shake. Are the spaces reinforcing that message, or working against it?

If some people sit in cubicles and others sit in corner offices, the space has already told everybody how much say they have. If the good conference room is technically free but the boss takes his calls in it half an hour a day, nobody else will use it. That room is sealed off. Not by a rule. By the space.

Leidy's argument is that the psychological needs of the people in a place come before any design decision. He names three: agency, growth and connection. Layout, paint colours and the technology on the wall all come after. Most organisations start with them.

The bit that surprised me was how much agency matters on its own. He talks about a study of nursing-home residents where the group who were allowed to change their own space were far more likely to be alive 18 months later. He is careful to say the size of that effect does not hold up across every study. His point is that having a say over your surroundings is one of the oldest forms of control we have, and small acts of it spill into the rest of life. Rearranging a room gets prescribed for depression for exactly that reason.

I once worked for a micromanager who took every ounce of that away. I remember being exhausted the whole time. Leidy's point is that an office can do the same thing quietly, without anybody deciding to.

The good news is that none of the fixes cost money.

Here is a ten-minute exercise for this week.

Walk your office, or scroll the photos if you are remote, and write down what the space is saying. Where do people actually gather? Which room is quietly off limits, and who decided? Where does the kitchen sit, and would anyone choose to stand there for five minutes? Then pick one thing you can change without a budget:

- Give a project team the room and let them set it up for the work.
- Move the couch nobody sits on in the lobby to where people already stop and talk.
- If a meeting matters and it is in a room you do not know, get there 20 minutes early. There is a negotiation study behind that one.

Leidy's smallest change for tomorrow is simpler still. Go somewhere new in your working environment.

We cut his full conversation into one eight-minute Better Moment. It covers the three needs, the nursing-home study, what a floor plan communicates and the moves that cost nothing.
