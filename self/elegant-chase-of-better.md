# The Elegant Chase of Better

The operating principle underneath everything. Not a tagline. The reason the system exists.

[[HWL Studio]]'s positioning is "turning [[Silent Excellence]] into visible leadership." That's the work. This is the why.

## Origin: Devonport, Auckland

Harrison grew up in Devonport, Auckland. Affluence was everywhere but he didn't go home to it. He was raised with not much, but he was raised with taste. That gap, seeing the standard without having the means, created a strategic, elegant dance between working his way into rooms and then showing up as if he belonged there.

"I saw what my parents had, was grateful for what they gave me, but I wanted more."

At 15, something broke. The thought patterns around him stopped making sense. He knew he could be something. That 15-year-old kid dared to dream for better.

"If only he could see me now. I 100% know he would say you should be screaming from the fucking rooftops about how you've made it. But I'm still screaming at myself from the inside saying how much better I can be."

## The Paradox

The 15-year-old's question was: "Why the fuck can't it be me?"

The 25-year-old's answer is: it is you. You're in the rooms. You're advising a £5bn company. You're running ultras. You're building something real. But the goalposts keep shifting. Every achievement becomes the new floor, not the ceiling. This success is the minimum viable acceptance.

## The Psychological Architecture

Seven mechanisms, one system. Unpacked on 5 April 2026 during a post-run reflection.

### 1. Relative Deprivation: The Sharpest Ambition Comes From Proximity

Stouffer (1949) identified that satisfaction tracks not absolute conditions but the perceived gap between what you have and what your reference group has. Runciman formalised four conditions, the critical one being: you believe obtaining it is possible.

Walker & Smith (2002): *egoistic deprivation* (personal, not group-based) predicts individual ambition and risk-taking. Crosby (1976) added that proximity is the accelerant. Deprivation felt most acutely isn't between distant groups but between people who share a social space. The closer to the wealth, the sharper the felt gap.

**What this means for Harrison:** A working-class kid in a working-class area doesn't develop this intensity. But Devonport, going to the houses, seeing the life, then going home to something different, that produces taste-calibrated ambition. Not chasing abstract wealth. Chasing a specific quality of life observed at close range. That's why HWL gravitates toward premium positioning, not volume.

**The shadow:** The reference group keeps updating upward. At 15, Devonport families. At 25, London professionals. At 35, it could be clients worth nine figures. The gap never closes because the comparison set keeps shifting.

**The lever:** Awareness that the drive is referential, not absolute, lets you consciously choose your reference group. Periodic audit: who am I comparing against, and is that comparison still useful?

### 2. Code-Switching: Performing Belonging Until It Becomes Real

Goffman (1959) treats all social interaction as performance, but code-switching under class pressure adds cognitive load. You're performing while monitoring whether the performance is passing. Bourdieu called the effortless version *habitus*: dispositions absorbed from your class environment. When your habitus doesn't match the field, you consciously perform what others do unconsciously.

Lubrano (2004) coined "straddlers" for people who cross class lines. Granfield (1991): working-class students at elite law schools developed "identity management strategies," suppressing markers of origin while adopting new ones. Jack (2014) at Harvard: low-income students proximate to affluence arrived with code-switching already operational. They could navigate elite spaces. But the switching itself is the tax.

Stephens, Fryberg & Markus (2012) at Stanford: first-generation students from working-class backgrounds performed worse in environments emphasising independence but equal or better in environments emphasising contribution. The working-class cognitive toolkit is collaborative, not deficient.

**What this means for Harrison:** Advising a £5bn company's C-suite at 25 requires real-time code-switching at speed. The skill is genuinely developed adaptive intelligence. But it costs cognitive resources that the person who learned the code natively doesn't spend. This creates both an advantage (conscious awareness of social dynamics that natives are blind to) and a cost (fatigue, hypervigilance about belonging).

**The lever:** The outsider's ear for what sounds authentic versus performed is sharper precisely because you had to learn the difference the hard way. That conscious awareness of social codes is literally the product you sell.

### 3. The Curse of Competence: Standards Outpace Confidence

Brickman & Campbell (1971): the hedonic treadmill. Ben-Shahar: the "arrival fallacy," the belief that reaching a goal will produce lasting satisfaction, followed by disorientation when it doesn't. His Harvard research found this particularly acute in high performers who structured identity around goal pursuit. The goal isn't just a target. It's a meaning-making structure. Achieving it removes the structure.

Higgins' Regulatory Focus Theory: people from resource-scarce backgrounds often develop a hybrid: **promotion-oriented in ambition but prevention-oriented in self-evaluation**. Chase gains aggressively while feeling that what you've achieved could be taken away. Each achievement is less "I've arrived" and more "I haven't fallen yet."

Chris Williamson's "curse of competence": the better you become, the more your standards outpace your performance. Maps onto Kruger & Dunning inverted: high competence paired with high self-awareness produces systematic undervaluation.

**What this means for Harrison:** "This success is my minimum viable acceptance" is the arrival fallacy married to prevention-focused self-evaluation. The £5bn client, the London life, the ultra training: not experienced as achievements but as the baseline below which failure begins.

**The shadow:** Chronic non-arrival leads to self-handicapping (Berglas & Jones): unconsciously sabotaging success to avoid the discomfort of achieving it and still feeling empty. Also burnout. If nothing registers as "enough," rest feels like regression.

**The lever:** Physical suffering creates present-moment experience that resists adaptation. You can't habituate to kilometre 60. The ultra training is already building some of this landing gear.

### 4. The Break at 15: Rejecting the Script

Erikson placed Identity vs Role Confusion at ages 12-18. Most adolescents resolve by conforming to available models. Some resolve by rejecting them and constructing alternative possible selves.

Markus & Nurius (1986): **"possible selves"** are cognitive representations of what you could become. Not fantasies. Self-regulatory mechanisms that organise behaviour toward specific futures. Oyserman (2006): adolescents with detailed, strategy-linked possible selves showed dramatically better outcomes, particularly among low-income students.

The critical variable: exposure to alternative models. Lareau (2003): working-class families emphasise conformity, affluent environments emphasise autonomy. A working-class kid inside an affluent ecosystem gets both messages. In some adolescents, this resolves as: "The limits my parents accept are not my limits."

Why Harrison and not every Devonport kid? Rotter (1966): internal locus of control. Dweck (2006): growth mindset. The belief that outcomes result from your own actions plus the belief that ability is malleable predicts willingness to attempt things your background says you shouldn't.

**The shadow:** Friedman & Laurison (2019), *The Class Ceiling*: socially mobile people often feel they belong fully in neither origin class nor destination class. The break that enables ambition severs a source of belonging.

**The lever:** The break doesn't need to be total. The most resilient class-mobile individuals maintain connection to origin values (work ethic, resourcefulness, non-entitlement) while operating in the new context. The origin isn't something to escape. It's the foundation the new identity is built on.

### 5. Taste Without Means: The Observer's Advantage

Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979): taste as class marker. But he underexplored a third path: taste through proximate observation without access. Not consuming. Not being taught. Watching, comparing, internalising quality distinctions from the outside.

Holt (1998): people with high cultural capital but low economic capital developed more **critical and analytical** relationships with aesthetics than either the wealthy (who consumed unreflectively) or the poor without exposure. The observer develops taste as a critical faculty rather than a consumption habit.

**What this means for Harrison:** "Anti-cringy sophistication" is an observer's aesthetic. Defined by what it rejects (performance, excess, trying too hard) as much as what it affirms. The taste fingerprint of someone who watched affluence from inside the radius but outside the gate. You learned to see what's real versus what's performed because you were never performing from comfort.

**The shadow:** Taste without means can become identity armour: using aesthetic judgment to feel superior to both the crass wealthy and the unsophisticated poor.

**The lever:** This is directly monetisable. Strategic communications for premium brands requires exactly this: distinguishing authentic quality from performed quality. The observer's eye is the product.

### 6. Gratitude and Hunger Are Parallel, Not Competing

Emmons & McCullough (2003): gratitude improves wellbeing. Carver & Scheier's self-regulation theory: when gratitude coexists with ambition, they operate on parallel channels. They don't cancel out. They oscillate.

Adler & Fagley (2005): **appreciative gratitude** (valuing what exists) is sustainable alongside ambition. **Comparative gratitude** (feeling fortunate relative to others) is fragile because any upward comparison flips it to resentment.

Tesser (1988): when someone close to you performs below your standards in a domain relevant to your identity, you experience a threat that can manifest as guilt for surpassing them. Gratitude toward parents while exceeding their life outcomes requires separating their worth as people from their achievements as benchmarks.

**What this means for Harrison:** "For better or for worse, it's what made me." That's appreciative, not comparative. The foundation and the building address different things. The sustainable version is: gratitude for the foundation, ambition for the building.

**The flip point:** When gratitude becomes obligation. "I should be grateful" shifts from appreciation to guilt, and guilt about wanting more suppresses ambition into resentment. You're not there. The honesty protects you.

### 7. Achievement Undervaluation: Not Impostor Syndrome

Clance & Imes (1978): impostor syndrome is believing you'll be exposed as incompetent. This is different. Genuine belief that the achievement, which you know you earned, just isn't significant.

Phillips & Lowery (2018): upwardly mobile individuals from low-status origins reported **lower subjective status** than people with equivalent objective achievements from high-status origins. Same job, same salary, same office. Different internal experience.

Jost & Banaji (1994), System Justification Theory: people from disadvantaged backgrounds often internalise the hierarchy's legitimacy. You don't feel like a fraud. You feel like the system's rewards are correctly calibrated and you just haven't done enough yet.

Destin & Oyserman (2009): students from low-income backgrounds primed with upward mobility cues worked harder but reported lower satisfaction with current achievements. The aspiration itself suppresses the sense of accomplishment.

**What this means for Harrison:** Systematic undervaluation leads to overwork, under-pricing (Creepers retainer undersold, Colin Fisher held at £1,500), and inability to rest.

**The lever:** The correction isn't affirmation. It's calibration. At 25, advising C-suite at a £5bn company is not a minimum. It's a statistical anomaly. Treating it as the floor is useful for drive but inaccurate as self-assessment. Both things can be true. The drive can continue without the self-assessment being warped.

## The Synthesis

These seven patterns are a single architecture:

| Mechanism | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| Relative deprivation | The **fuel** |
| Code-switching | The **vehicle** |
| Hedonic adaptation | Prevents the **destination** from arriving |
| The adolescent break | Set the **trajectory** |
| Observational taste | Shaped what **success looks like** |
| Gratitude-hunger tension | Keeps the engine from **stalling or redlining** |
| Achievement undervaluation | Keeps the **foot on the accelerator** |

**The central risk: the system that got you here is optimised for pursuit, not for arrival. Every mechanism is a launch system. None of them are landing gear.**

The work is not building more drive. It's building the capacity to experience what the drive has already produced. Not instead of the chase. Alongside it.

## What To Do With This

Three practices, not a system:

1. **Name the patterns in real time.** When you under-price, that's achievement undervaluation. When you dismiss LOR as "just where I'm at," that's the hedonic treadmill. Naming creates a half-second gap between the pattern firing and acting on it.

2. **Audit the reference group quarterly.** Who are you comparing against? Would a rational person in your position choose that benchmark? If you're measuring against people with 15 years on you, you've found the distortion.

3. **The 15-year-old gets a letter.** Eventually. An honest accounting. What you built, what it cost, what surprised you, what he'd be proud of, what you're still figuring out. That's a piece of [[Personal Content]] unlike anything else on the internet.
