# Content Frameworks Reference

Source frameworks for the `content-review` skill. Read this file before producing any content output via the skill.

## 1. The Five Content Games

Every piece of content should be mapped to one of five games based on the business model. The game determines idea selection, packaging strategy, audience breadth, and success metrics.

### Game 1 — Entertainment Media
- Purpose: Maximize views for CPM/ad revenue/brand deals.
- Strategy: Go wide within a niche, sensational topics, high production.
- Metric: Views, engagement rate, follower count (for brand deal pricing).
- Examples: MrBeast, streamers, tech news channels.
- Warning: This game is "racing to zero" as AI content floods supply. Not recommended as a starting point in 2025-2026.

### Game 2 — Education Media
- Purpose: Maximize views through tactically useful content.
- Key distinction from Game 1: Education = viewer can walk away and apply something to solve a problem. Everything else is entertainment, even if it feels educational.
- Strategy: Similar to Game 1 but content must be implementable.
- Often transitions into Game 5 once an offer is built.

### Game 3 — Commoditized Consumer Product
- The product is self-explanatory (clothing, furniture, food).
- Strategy: Less context needed in content. Show the product, create desire visually.
- Content can be UGC-heavy, aesthetic-focused.

### Game 4 — Consumer Product Requiring Education
- Product needs explanation (supplements, tech gadgets, SaaS for consumers).
- Strategy: Bridge the education gap quickly, show what it is, what it does, why it matters.
- Content must educate AND create desire.

### Game 5 — B2B Products & Services
- Coaching, consulting, agencies, info products, B2B SaaS.
- Strategy: Build a personal brand around expertise. Content must demonstrate ability to get results.
- Requires the most trust (most content minutes) before conversion.
- Ideal approach: High-value, non-obvious, tactically implementable content on YouTube.
- "Non-obvious" = things the viewer wouldn't figure out on their own. "Tactically implementable" = they can apply it immediately.

The fundamental formula: Business = Traffic + Funnels + Offers. You either start with an offer and go get traffic, or start building traffic (content) and figure out the offer later. Most creators start with traffic, get addicted to the views game, go too broad, and then can't convert when they finally build an offer.

## 2. Content Minutes

The most important framework in all of content strategy.

The model: To convert, a viewer must accumulate a threshold of watched minutes. Think of each minute as one block.

- Email lead magnet conversion: ~4 blocks
- $97 product: ~12 blocks
- $10,000 product: ~400 blocks

The implication: You need to maximise both the rate of block accumulation (binge-ability) and the efficiency of each block (value density).

Binge-ability drivers:
- Numbered series (e.g., "Day 4 of 10"), triggers the brain's puzzle-completion instinct
- Thumbnail covers on profile pages, give context so viewers can self-select what to watch next
- Chain reactions, one piece leads naturally into the next
- YouTube playlists and end screens

Why YouTube wins for trust: Average YouTube video: 25 min video, ~10 min watched = 10 blocks per rep. Average short: 60s, ~30s watched = 0.5 blocks per rep. It takes 20 shorts to equal one YouTube video in trust accrual. Plus YouTube is intent-based (viewer chose to click) vs feed-based (passively served). Intent = higher quality blocks.

Why shorts still matter: Shorts are discovery engines. They get eyeballs to the profile. The profile (with good covers/thumbnails) drives them to long form. Long form accrues the trust. It's a pipeline, not a single channel.

## 3. The Hook Framework

For any type of content to work, the viewer must stop and decide to watch. The hook is where this happens.

The three elements of a hook (short form):

1. **Visual Hook** — what's shown on screen. This acts first (speed of light > speed of sound). Its job is to "stun", create a thumb-stop through contrast with the typical feed.
2. **Text Hook** — text overlaid on the video. Two types:
   - Title text: Big words that set context or create curiosity gap. Not word-for-word what's being said.
   - Captions: The actual spoken words, styled and displayed.
3. **Spoken Hook** — the words actually said. Should be: fewest words possible, active voice, maximum clarity.

Critical rule: All three must be aligned. If the visual shows one thing, the text says another, and the speaker says a third, confusion. Confusion = churn. The opposite of confusion is comprehension. The viewer's brain is trying to triangulate meaning across all three channels. When they align, there's a "click", the viewer confirms their assumption, absorption is high, and they're locked in.

The hook sequence:
1. Visual stuns → viewer pauses for a moment
2. Context is set → viewer understands what this is about (top-down processing: broad context first)
3. Contrast is delivered → curiosity fires, viewer stays to learn more

## 4. Absorption Rate

The most important KPI in content is absorption rate: the percentage of your content that the viewer actually understands and retains.

If you say 8 words, does the viewer understand all 8? Can they put them together and be 100% certain this only means one thing? That's 100% absorption.

The tension: Cutting words increases speed-to-value but can decrease absorption if you cut clarity-critical words. Adding words increases absorption but decreases speed-to-value if they're fluff.

The rule: KPI is clarity first, compression second. Use the fewest words necessary to achieve 100% absorption. Cut fluff ruthlessly, but never cut a word that carries clarity.

How to audit absorption:
- Read each sentence. Is there only one possible interpretation?
- If someone asked "what do you mean by that?", you've failed absorption on that line.
- The "eyes closed test" (for video): close your eyes and just listen. Can you follow every idea perfectly? If you're confused or bored, there's an absorption problem.

Comprehension decay model: Comprehension starts at 100% on the first frame. Every word, visual, and frame can only maintain or decrease it. At some threshold, the viewer is so confused they leave. The goal is to keep comprehension as high as possible for as long as possible. Misaligned visuals, unclear sentences, wrong pacing, all accelerate decay.

## 5. The Seven Lego Bricks of Short Form

Every short form video is composed of seven elements. To create content, figure out what's winning in the target niche, decompose the best videos into these seven bricks, hold constant what's working, and differentiate on the rest.

1. **Topic** — What subject matter is covered. Changes per video.
2. **Angle** — The specific take or framing on the topic. Changes per video. This is where "non-obvious" lives.
3. **Hook Structure** — How the opening is constructed (the visual/text/spoken alignment). Can be held constant across videos.
4. **Story Structure** — How the information is laid out: case study, breakdown, listicle, narrative, tutorial, debate. Can be held constant.
5. **Visual Format** — How the visuals are arranged on screen: split screen, full screen, circle overlay, vlog POV, B-roll driven, in-the-world shots. Can be held constant.
6. **Key Visuals** — The specific visual assets used: A-roll (you on camera), B-roll, graphics, screen recordings. Can be varied.
7. **Audio** — Music and sound effects. Can be held constant.

For beginners: Hold 5-6 bricks constant (copy what's working in your niche) and vary Topic and Angle. This reduces unknown unknowns and accelerates learning.

For advanced creators: Go back to the bricks you were holding constant and ask: can I do this in a way no one has done? Can I borrow from another industry and remix it? This is how you create signature formats.

## 6. Contrast — The Engine of Curiosity

Contrast is the distance between what the viewer expects and what you show them. It's the primary driver of curiosity, which is what drives attention.

Direct contrast: Explicitly state the common belief, then flip it.
- "Most people think hooks are about [X]. The truth is they have nothing to do with what you've been taught."
- This creates maximum contrast distance: you push the viewer to +100 (confirming their belief), then flip them to -100. Net distance: 200.

Implied contrast: Just state the surprising thing without establishing the common belief first.
- "Cars are driving backwards on the road and nobody knows why."
- The viewer has to construct the common belief themselves (cars normally drive forward). They start at 0 and move to -100. Net distance: 100.

Direct contrast is almost always stronger because it gives context first (aligning with how the brain processes information: top-down, broad to specific) and creates a larger contrast arc.

The Shock Score: Rate any insight on a scale of 0-100: out of 100 people in a room, how many would have already believed this? The fewer who would have predicted it, the higher the shock score, the more compelling the contrast.

Using contrast in storytelling: The viewer is constantly guessing what comes next. You either confirm their guess (satisfying) or reveal something they didn't expect (curiosity-inducing). Great storytelling zigzags between the two, confirm enough to build trust, surprise enough to maintain curiosity.

## 7. The Question Chain

The simplest and most powerful framework for auditing story flow in any content.

The principle: Each line of content should open exactly one clear question in the viewer's mind. The next line answers that question and opens a new one. If you chain this correctly all the way through, the story flow is perfect.

Example:
- "Google just dropped Gemini 3, a crazy AI model." → Question: What can it do?
- "It can generate full applications from a single prompt." → Question: How do I use it?
- "If you want to use it, go to this URL." → Question: How much does it cost?
- "And if you go here, you can actually get a discount." → Question chain complete.

How to audit: Read each line. What's the one question it triggers? If you can't identify it, the line is unclear. If it triggers multiple equally strong questions, narrow it down, confusion is the enemy.

For short form (60s): You can fit roughly 3 story loops/sections. An opening "holy shit what is this" section, a breakdown/how-it-works section, and an implication/takeaway section.

## 8. Audience Matching & Algorithm Mechanics

The algorithm's only job is to keep people on the platform watching (more watching = more ads = more revenue). To do this, it needs to predict what each user wants to see. Your content strategy must work with this.

How the algorithm samples:
1. You post a video. The platform transcribes it and builds a topic map.
2. It creates a sample pool (~200 people), some existing followers, some new.
3. It assesses: did these people watch/engage more than usual, about the same, or less?
4. If more → build a new sample pool at 10x (2,000), similar makeup. Repeat.
5. If same or less → try another cohort or throttle.

Why topic consistency matters: If your videos cover tech, then science, then basketball, your follower base is fragmented. When you post a new video, only 1/3 of the sample pool cares. The engagement signal comes back weak. The algorithm throttles. Even if the content is individually excellent, the mismatched audience kills distribution.

The rule: Pick one topic and hammer it. All content pillars should serve the same ICP. You can go wider within the topic (for the media game) or stay narrow (for the offer game), but never leave the topic.

Transcription-based SEO: Platforms transcribe your content in real time and build a contextual map. Using specific terminology (long-tail keywords) in your spoken words helps the algorithm correctly categorise and distribute your content. It's like tagging blog posts, the video IS a long-tail blog post.

Minimum credibility thresholds: Followers/subscribers matter only in a binary sense, above or below a minimum credibility line. Below it, viewers won't trust you regardless of content quality. Above it, the specific number doesn't matter much. Rough thresholds: Twitter ~2K, Instagram ~10K, YouTube ~10K subs.

## 9. YouTube Packaging — Title, Thumbnail, Intro

YouTube is a packaging game more than a content game. If nobody clicks, the video might as well be a black screen.

The Desire Loop:
1. Viewer scrolling → thumbnail catches eye (visual stun)
2. Viewer reads title → triggers a desire (pain, FOMO, or benefit)
3. Viewer looks back at thumbnail → amplifies the desire
4. Click.

Title rules:
- Must trigger a desire loop, the viewer needs to feel pain (this problem is costing me), FOMO (I'm missing out), or desire (I want that result).
- Don't repeat what's in the thumbnail. The title and thumbnail are complementary canvases.
- Aim for clarity over cleverness. The viewer needs to instantly know what they'll get.

Thumbnail rules:
- Complement and amplify the title's promise, don't repeat the same words.
- Must be visually distinctive in a sea of thumbnails.
- Design the thumbnail FIRST, before scripting. This forces you to validate the idea's clickability before investing time.

Intro rules:
- First 5 seconds must confirm the expectation set by the title/thumbnail. This is not the time for backstory or slow build.
- Get to the first "dopamine hit" (a genuinely useful or surprising insight) within 30-60 seconds.
- After the first dopamine hit, the viewer has largely forgotten the title/thumbnail, you've successfully "launched the rocket" and discarded the booster.

The Two Dopamine Hit Rule: If you can deliver two genuine dopamine hits (non-obvious insights the viewer can use) before they would naturally churn, they're locked in for the long haul. Zero hits = bounce. One hit = maybe. Two hits = committed.

Bait vs Bait-and-Switch: Clickbait is good, you WANT to bait the click. The sin is switching. If the title says "marketing cheat code" and the first 5 seconds are about marketing, you've baited and delivered. If the first 5 seconds are about something unrelated, you've baited and switched.

YouTube metrics that matter:
- For media game: 1-of-10 ranking (relative performance against your last 10 videos), CTR (noisy but directional).
- For business game: Dollars per view (total revenue attributed / views). A video with 300 views generating $300K > a video with 5M views generating $5K.
- Retention charts: Look for abnormal drop-offs. Usually caused by confusion (visuals misaligned, unclear sentence) or loss of interest (pacing too slow, no value delivery).

## 10. Storytelling for Conversion

The difference between stories that entertain and stories that convert is not the storytelling technique, it's the topic and what you cover.

Conversion comes from trust. Trust comes from proof and demonstration of ability.

If you're selling an agency service, your content must demonstrate that you can get results. If you're selling a product, your content must show the transformation. Storytelling is the vehicle, not the destination.

What makes content convert:
- It covers a topic directly relevant to the viewer's pain/desire
- It demonstrates the creator's expertise through non-obvious, implementable insights
- It provides enough "taste" of the result that the viewer believes buying will deliver more
- It's consistent enough in topic that trust compounds over time (content minutes)

Storytelling is the connective tissue: Contrast, stakes, relevance, these keep attention. But the meat must demonstrate ability. A perfectly told story about something irrelevant to the offer won't convert.

Narrative-driven storytelling (currently dominant format):
- Personal, one-of-one experiences structured as: I encountered this problem → here's what I tried (didn't work) → here's what actually worked → if you have a similar problem, here you go.
- This works because: (a) it's hard to copy (lived experiences are unique), (b) it demonstrates expertise through real results, (c) it's more relatable than "5 tips" content.
- This format is staying in vogue because one-of-one content is inherently resistant to commoditisation.

## 11. Visual Principles

Three attributes of high-performing visuals:
1. **High Motion** — The brain is wired to detect motion (evolutionary survival instinct). High motion = thumb stop. Static talking heads with minimal movement perform worst. Motion can be natural (action, walking, gestures) or artificial (zoom, push-in, overlays, transitions).
2. **High Colour** — Vibrant, saturated visuals pop on feed. Muted/dark tones blend in.
3. **High Contrast** — The visual must differentiate from typical feed content. If every podcast has wooden slats and a tree in the corner, a different set design contrasts. Contrast is relative to the niche, what stands out depends on what's normal.

Relative to niche maturity: In a mature niche (tech/AI), the visual bar is very high, you need extremely dialed visuals. In an immature niche (dentistry), even basic motion and colour can be enough because the competition is low.

Visual alignment: The visual shown on screen must match what's being said. If you say "this robot has human tendons" and show the robot, the viewer's brain clicks, assumption confirmed, absorption high. If you show something unrelated, confusion accelerates.

## 12. Editing Principles

The eyes-closed test: After completing a video edit, close your eyes and just listen. If you're bored → pacing is too slow, tighten the cuts. If you can't follow → pacing is too fast or words are unclear, add breathing room.

Pacing: The speed of cuts. Too slow = viewer gets bored. Too fast = viewer can't absorb. The right pace matches the complexity of the content, dense information needs slightly more breathing room than simple statements.

Visual selection: For each sentence, ask: what is the best possible way to visualise this? Sometimes it's just the speaker on camera. Sometimes it's B-roll that confirms and amplifies the words. Using the wrong visual at the wrong time is one of the biggest editing mistakes.

Comprehension in editing: Every cut, every overlay, every transition either maintains or degrades comprehension. Unnecessary complexity (flashy effects that don't serve meaning) is anti-absorption.

## 13. Content Workflow & Operations

The six-step content production loop: Ideas → Research → Scripting → Recording → Editing → Posting

**Ideas** (hardest to systematise):
- Scan "idea pipes": other creators in the niche, X/Twitter, Reddit, YouTube, newsletters.
- Curate a shortlist: what's interesting, time-sensitive, high contrast potential?
- Pitch call: the ideas person pitches ideas with angles, visual possibilities, and cons. The decision-maker filters.
- This is the bottleneck, ideas are a bottomless pit. Getting this off the plate is the highest-leverage operational move.

**Scripting:**
- Start with the angle: what's non-obvious about this topic? What do most people believe that's wrong?
- Write the hook first: context + contrast, all three elements aligned.
- Apply the question chain: each line answers the previous question and opens a new one.
- Audit for absorption: sentence by sentence, is this clear on first read?

**Recording:**
- Currently hard to automate without quality loss. AI avatars exist but don't save meaningful time.
- Focus on energy, pacing, and clarity of delivery.

**Editing:**
- Can be systematised with trained editors + templates + AI tools for B-roll generation.
- Give editors autonomy to build systems on off-days.
- The eyes-closed test is the final quality gate.

**Posting:**
- For short form: thumbnail covers matter for profile browsing and DM sharing.
- For YouTube: packaging (title/thumbnail) should be finalised before the video is posted, ideally before scripting.
- Numbering series increases binge-ability but commits you to a cadence, have 5 episodes stacked before starting.

## 14. Distribution & Virality

The biggest myth about virality: you don't need it.

On-target views (views from people who match your ICP and might convert) are infinitely more valuable than total views. Dollar per view is the real metric.

Virality mechanics:
- Virality comes from shares. The viral K-factor: 100 see it → 3 share → each share reaches 5 → 1 of those 5 shares → loop continues.
- DM shares on Instagram: the video sits as a thumbnail cover. If the cover is bad, fewer people click it even in a trusted group chat. Good covers increase share-to-view conversion.
- "Super shares": shares/likes from high-follower accounts disproportionately boost algorithmic distribution.
- Comments as engagement drivers: use scripts to drive contrarian debate in comments. Fighting in comments = high engagement signal = algorithmic boost.

Followers as credibility proxy:
- Followers matter in binary (above or below credibility threshold) and for brand deal pricing.
- The difference between 80K and 365K followers is functionally nothing for conversion.
- The only incremental follower worth having is one who's on-target for your offer.

## 15. Format Evolution & The AI Wave

The format maturity cycle:
- Early platform (2020-2022): Low-effort transactional formats work (listicles, tutorials, "5 best tools"). Supply is low.
- Mid-maturity (2022-2024): Case study breakdowns rise. More original context required to differentiate.
- Current era (2025-2026): Personal, narrative-driven storytelling dominates. One-of-one experiences that can't be commoditised.

Why narrative-driven storytelling is enduring: Lived experiences are unique by definition. You can copy a "5 tips" format but you can't copy someone's specific story of building and selling a company. Harder to execute but harder to replicate.

The AI content wave:
- AI content will replace the most commoditised formats first (text tweets → short lists → simple explainers → eventually longer form).
- The window for free organic distribution is approximately 2-3 years remaining (closing ~2028).
- Content that survives: niche, one-of-one, human-verified, long-form, live.
- The moat: signature experiences, deep niche expertise, proof/credibility that can't be faked.

The new cancellation: Being caught using AI for content. Everyone wants AI speed, but detection = soft cancellation. The solution: use AI for compression and combination (taking inputs and synthesising), not for ideation (generating novel thoughts from scratch).

## 16. The Iterative Improvement Model

Volume negates everything. Strategy is commoditised, anyone can learn the frameworks. The actual differentiator is: can you make one rep, then iterate off it, closing gaps faster than anyone else?

The beginner's trap: People try to batch content before they understand fundamentals. Batching lengthens iteration cycles. When you batch 5, you make the same mistake from video 1 through video 3 because you didn't get feedback.

The right approach (beginners):
1. Make one piece. Post it. See the data.
2. Use the Lego Brick framework to audit: which of the seven elements was weak?
3. Pick 1-2 things to improve. Make the next piece. Repeat.
4. After ~300 reps with iterative learning, you know what to look for and can start batching.

The right approach (advanced):
- Batch 2-3, then 5-10 at a time.
- Use operators/editors to handle the known-good parts.
- Focus personal time on the creative decisions that can't be systematised: idea selection, angle development, taste.

The fork in the road: Most people quit at 10-30 reps when they realise it's going to take 500 reps to get good. The ones who make it are egoless about learning, can close multiple gaps per rep, and simply don't stop.

## 17. Lead Magnets & Owned Audience

Moving people from social (rented audience) to owned audience (email list, community) is critical for conversion.

Types of lead magnets (ranked by friction):
1. **One-off PDF/doc:** "Here's my storytelling framework, download link in bio." Lowest friction, highest conversion, lowest retention.
2. **3-5 day email series:** Delivers value over multiple days. Builds habit. Higher retention.
3. **Newsletter:** Ongoing value delivery. Converts subscribers over time through consistent content minutes accrual.
4. **Free tool:** Software or calculator that solves a specific problem. Highest perceived value.

The mechanism: Content creates awareness → lead magnet captures email → email nurtures with more value → offer is made when trust threshold is met.

The key principle: The lead magnet must be directly relevant to both the content topic AND the eventual offer. If your content is about marketing but your offer is about sales, the lead magnet needs to bridge the two.
